Abstract

Recent Continuous Thought Machine (CTM) architecture decouples internal computation from external inputs via neural dynamics, but relies on unconstrained multi-layer perceptrons for its recurrent computations. We propose to model neural dynamics using asymmetric Excitatory-Inhibitory (E-I) networks, which can be stabilized via principles from network theory, while obeying Dale's principle and can be expressed as energy-based systems optimized through a game-theoretic loss. Building on this perspective, we introduce Temporal Inhibitory-Excitatory Dynamic Engine (TIDE), a neuro-inspired architecture that computes internal representations through neural dynamics stabilized by incorporating the Wilson-Cowan dynamics and lateral inhibition. TIDE balances biological realism by, for instance, using Hierarchical Receptive Fields and enforcing Dale's principle to ensure a realistic 80 : 20 E-I balance ratio with an end-to-end trainable architecture. The aim of this paper is to introduce a new architecture that brings neuro-inspired learning and architectural principles to the forefront. We present proofs of convergence, stability, and complexity bounds, along with empirical ablation studies. Overall, TIDE achieves higher top-1 accuracy than CTM on the majority of the investigated datasets while requiring a smaller training budget. Furthermore, across varying training budgets, TIDE improves top-1 accuracy on ImageNet-C by an average of +1.65% to +6.42% under different perturbation severity levels.

Method

TIDE keeps the loop of the Continuous Thought Machine, a sequence of internal steps that is decoupled from the input, and changes what runs inside it. The neurons are split 80:20 into an excitatory and an inhibitory population. Every recurrent weight is non-negative, and whether a connection excites or inhibits is decided by the population it leaves, which is Dale's principle. The two populations follow a forward-Euler Wilson-Cowan update with their own time constants.

Training and stability

Training under Dale's principle
Projected gradient descent: after every optimizer step the recurrent weights are clamped to W ≥ 0.
Game-theoretic loss
The E-I circuit is read as a game between neurons, and the loss penalizes the fixed-point residual of the per-neuron energies (Betteti et al., 2025).
Stability
Lyapunov diagonal stability is monitored during training through a differentiable Perron sum-ratio surrogate that caps ρ(WEE) and ρ(WII).

Robustness

Both models are trained on clean ImageNet-1K only and then evaluated on ImageNet-C, which applies 19 corruption types at five severities. At 500K training steps TIDE is ahead of CTM by 2.68 points on average over all 95 cells. With smaller training budgets the gap is wider: 16.8 points at 100K steps and 6.4 points at 200K. CTM is the more robust of the two under fog, frost and snow.

Limitations

The main limitation of both TIDE and CTM is their required sequential feedforward data processing, which prohibits shorter runtime and prevents more robust investigation. Therefore, further investigation is required to assess the feasibility of leveraging neuromorphic hardware and its dynamics, not only to enhance data preprocessing but also to implement a Look-up-Table-based approach to further reduce the training duration of the proposed framework. Given that we have already lowered the training boundary by around 50% to achieve results comparable to CTM, indicating the soundness of our approach, further studies are still needed to assess the reachability of an even sparser representation. Additionally, due to the lack of comparable implementations using similar approaches, detailed benchmarking within the same architectural family remains limited and challenging. Although CTM uses LSTM as its baseline, it is not a direct comparison; however, their results can be directly translated to TIDE given that we use CTM as our baseline.
